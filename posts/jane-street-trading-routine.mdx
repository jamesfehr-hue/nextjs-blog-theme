Jane Street is one of the world's most influential quantitative trading firms — a global market maker operating across more than 200 venues in 45+ countries. They trade ETFs, equities, bonds, options, and crypto, not by predicting the market, but by exploiting small, repeatable pricing inefficiencies at massive scale.

What makes Jane Street worth studying isn't their size. It's their **discipline**. Every decision is data-driven. Every trade has a defined risk. Every strategy is measured, iterated, and improved. That process is something any serious investor can learn from.

This post breaks Jane Street's approach down into a **practical daily and weekly routine** you can follow. Review each section, ask questions, and refine the routine to fit your goals.

---

## The Four Pillars of the Jane Street Approach

Before the routine, understand the philosophy it is built on.

### 1. Market Making & Arbitrage

Jane Street profits by quoting both a buy price (bid) and a sell price (ask) simultaneously, capturing the **spread** between them thousands of times per day. They also look for the same asset priced differently in two places and buy the cheaper one while selling the expensive one — that is arbitrage.

**What you take from this:** You may not be a market maker, but you can adopt the arbitrage mindset. Always ask: *Is this asset priced correctly relative to its substitutes?* A stock trading at a discount to its sector peers, or an ETF trading at a premium to its underlying net asset value (NAV), is a signal worth investigating.

### 2. Machine Learning & Big Data

Jane Street feeds algorithms with enormous datasets — order flow, volatility surfaces, macro indicators, earnings history — to detect relationships invisible to the naked eye. Their edge is not a gut feeling; it is a **quantified signal**.

**What you take from this:** You do not need a supercomputer. You need a structured way to track and measure your own signals. Keep a trading journal. Record *why* you entered each trade and *whether that reason played out*. Over time, you will learn which of your instincts are actually edges and which are noise.

### 3. Risk Management

Jane Street does not take naked bets. Every position is hedged. If they buy an ETF, they often short the underlying basket of stocks to isolate the specific mispricing they are targeting. They size positions so that no single trade can blow up the portfolio.

**What you take from this:** Define your maximum loss *before* you enter any trade. Use position sizing rules. Never let one idea represent more than a set percentage of your portfolio. Losses are not failure — *unplanned* losses are.

### 4. Collaborative Problem-Solving

Jane Street runs a flat structure where traders, engineers, and researchers openly debate ideas. No idea is sacred. Strategies that stop working are retired. This creates **rapid feedback loops**: small experiments, careful measurement, fast iteration.

**What you take from this:** Treat your portfolio like a research project. Weekly reviews, honest scoring of your decisions, and willingness to exit ideas that are not working are what separate disciplined investors from gamblers.

## The Daily Routine

### Pre-Market (Before 9:30 AM ET)

**Step 1 — Macro Context Check (15 minutes)**

Before looking at any individual stock, understand the environment you are trading in.

- Check overnight futures (S&P 500, Nasdaq, Dow)
- Note major economic data releases scheduled for the day (CPI, FOMC, jobs report)
- Scan headlines for geopolitical or sector-specific developments
- Check the VIX (volatility index): above 20 means elevated fear, above 30 means potential dislocation

*Jane Street principle at work:* They always know the macro regime before deploying capital. Spreads widen and risks change during volatile periods.

**Step 2 — Watchlist Review (15 minutes)**

Maintain a curated watchlist of 10–20 names you understand well. Each morning:

- Flag any with news or earnings overnight
- Note which are near key support/resistance levels
- Identify any ETF vs. stock discrepancies (e.g., sector ETF up but a component stock lagging)

**Step 3 — Set Your Daily Risk Budget**

Decide, *in writing*, before the market opens:

- Maximum loss you will accept today (e.g., 1% of portfolio)
- Maximum number of new positions you will open
- Any names you are avoiding today due to upcoming catalysts

If you hit your daily loss limit, you stop trading. No exceptions. This is the most important rule in the routine.

---

### Market Hours (9:30 AM – 4:00 PM ET)

**Step 4 — Avoid the First 15 Minutes**

The first 15 minutes of trading are chaotic. Order flow is imbalanced, spreads are wide, and prices whipsaw. Jane Street-style thinking says: *let the noise settle before committing capital*. Watch, do not trade, from 9:30 to 9:45.

**Step 5 — Look for ETF/Stock Dislocations**

One of Jane Street's core ETF arbitrage plays is buying an ETF when it trades at a discount to its NAV and selling (or shorting) it at a premium. You can track NAV discounts and premiums on most broker platforms or at sites that publish intraday ETF data.

As an individual investor, a simpler version of this is:

- If a quality sector ETF is down 2% but its top five holdings are each down only 0.5%, the ETF may be oversold relative to the components. That gap tends to close.
- Watch for mean-reversion setups where a single stock has diverged sharply from its correlated peers.

**Step 6 — Execute with Defined Entries and Exits**

Before placing any trade, write down three things:

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Entry price | $142.50 |
| Stop-loss price | $139.00 (2.4% below entry) |
| Target price | $150.00 (5.2% above entry) |

Your reward-to-risk ratio should be at least 2:1. If your target is not at least twice as far from entry as your stop, the trade does not have a Jane Street-caliber edge.

**Step 7 — Position Sizing**

Use a consistent position sizing formula:

```
Risk per trade = 1% of portfolio
Position size = Risk per trade ÷ (Entry price − Stop price)
```

Example: $50,000 portfolio × 1% = $500 risk. Entry $142.50, stop $139.00, so risk per share = $3.50. Position size = $500 ÷ $3.50 = ~142 shares.

This ensures one bad trade cannot meaningfully damage your portfolio.

---

### Post-Market (After 4:00 PM ET)

**Step 8 — Daily Journal Entry (10 minutes)**

Record every trade in a structured log:

- Ticker, entry, exit, P&L
- *Why* you entered (the signal or thesis)
- *Did the thesis play out?* (Even if you lost money, the thesis may have been correct — or vice versa)
- What you would do differently

This journal is your data. Over months, patterns will emerge. You will see which signals actually predict outcomes and which are illusions.

**Step 9 — Rebalance Your Risk**

At the end of each day:

- If a position moved significantly in your favor, consider tightening your stop to lock in partial gains
- If a position is approaching your stop, decide now whether you will honor it tomorrow — do not leave this decision to the heat of the moment
- Check that no single position now represents an outsized share of your portfolio

## The Weekly Review (Every Weekend)

This is the most Jane Street-like step in the entire routine. It is where rapid feedback loops happen.

**Step 10 — Score Your Decisions, Not Your Returns**

Ask yourself, for each trade closed this week:

1. Was the thesis clearly defined before entry? (Yes / No)
2. Did I follow my position sizing rules? (Yes / No)
3. Did I honor my stop-loss? (Yes / No)
4. Did I take profit at my target or hold without a plan? (Disciplined / Undisciplined)

A trade that lost money but scored 4/4 on process is a *good trade*. A trade that made money but scored 2/4 is a *warning sign* — you got lucky, not good.

**Step 11 — Retire What Is Not Working**

If a signal or strategy has underperformed over 10+ trades, retire it. Jane Street does this constantly. Markets evolve, edges decay. There is no shame in moving on — only in holding onto a broken idea.

**Step 12 — Study One New Concept**

Spend 30–60 minutes each week reading about one quantitative or options topic. A short list of Jane Street-relevant subjects:

- Options pricing and implied volatility
- ETF creation/redemption mechanics
- Statistical arbitrage basics
- Factor investing (value, momentum, quality)
- Probability and expected value

## What to Ask Yourself Next

This routine is a starting framework. The questions below are the ones a Jane Street-style trader would pressure-test it with. Use them to start a conversation, refine the routine, and make it your own:

1. **What signals am I actually tracking?** Do I have a written list of the conditions that trigger a trade, or am I improvising each time?
2. **What is my actual edge?** If I cannot describe in one sentence why I should win a specific trade on average, I probably should not take it.
3. **What does my journal tell me?** After 20+ trades, what patterns show up?
4. **Am I being honest about my stops?** Moving a stop lower "just this once" is how small losses become large ones.
5. **What would I do differently if I had to explain every trade to a partner?** The accountability of collaborative problem-solving is one of Jane Street's real advantages.
